# *Voluntārius*: one suffix, four endings

A study of a Latin adjective that English borrowed twice with different endings, and of a verb that almost no other language in Western Europe has managed to form at all.

---

## 1. The root

Latin *volō, velle* "to wish, want, choose" descends from Proto-Indo-European **\*welh₁-** "to wish, to choose." The root is shared with Germanic: English *will*, German *wollen*, and Old Norse *vilja* are all cognate with it, so that a *volunteer* and a *will* are the same word at four thousand years' distance.

One curiosity of the paradigm deserves mention. Latin *vel* "or" is the bare imperative of *volō* — literally "choose" — so that the ordinary Latin disjunction is an instruction to the listener to take their pick.

From *volō* Latin formed the abstract *voluntās* "will, wish, inclination, choice," and from that the adjective *voluntārius* "of one's own will, willing, voluntary."

---

## 2. The Roman volunteer

*Voluntārius* was substantivised early and in a specific setting. The *voluntāriī* were troops who presented themselves for service rather than being levied — volunteers as against conscripts — and the word appears in this sense throughout the military historians.

The English noun enters in exactly the same setting. It is a sixteenth-century military term for one who offers himself for service, and only later broadens to cover anyone who does anything unasked.

---

## 3. Four endings from one suffix

Latin *-ārius* is among the most productive suffixes in the language, and its reflexes reached English by several routes at several dates. English holds four of them:

| English ending | Route | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-ary* | learned, directly from Latin *-ārius* | *voluntary*, *contrary*, *necessary* |
| *-eer* | from French *-ier* | *volunteer*, *engineer*, *mutineer*, *pioneer* |
| *-ier* | from French *-ier*, unassimilated | *cavalier*, *brigadier*, *chandelier* |
| *-aire* | from French *-aire* | *millionaire*, *doctrinaire*, *questionnaire* |

*Voluntary* and *volunteer* are therefore a doublet: one Latin word, borrowed twice, differing only in which reflex of *-ārius* it arrived wearing. The learned form kept the adjective's function; the French form took the noun's.

English also disagrees with French about the stem vowel. Latin has *u* in *voluntās*; French lowered it, giving *volonté*, *volontaire*, and *volontiers*; Italian did the same with *volontario*. Spanish *voluntario*, Portuguese *voluntário*, and English *volunteer* show the Latin *u*. Since English took the word from French and then restored the Latin vowel while keeping the French suffix, the received spelling is a hybrid — Latin in the stem, French in the ending.

---

## 4. The verb almost nobody has

English converts the noun to a verb without ceremony: *to volunteer*, attested from the mid eighteenth century. This is unremarkable in English and nearly unique in the neighbourhood.

| | The noun | The adjective | The verb |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Latin** | *voluntārius* | *voluntārius* | — |
| **French** | *le volontaire* | *volontaire* | *se porter volontaire* |
| **Spanish** | *el voluntario* | *voluntario* | *ofrecerse voluntario* |
| **Portuguese** | *o voluntário* | *voluntário* | *voluntariar-se* |
| **Italian** | *il volontario* | *volontario* | *offrirsi volontario* |
| **English** | *the volunteer* | *voluntary* | *to volunteer* |
| **Inglisce** | *þa volontire* | *volontairy* | *to volontier* |

The first two columns are one word in every language but two: Latin, French, Spanish, Portuguese, and Italian each use a single form for the person and the quality, since *voluntārius* was an adjective substantivised and never stopped being an adjective. English separated them, and the separation is the doublet described above — the noun took the French ending and the adjective the Latin one.

French, Spanish, and Italian have no verb at all. Each builds a phrase around a different verb of offering or bearing — *se porter*, *ofrecerse*, *offrirsi* — with *volontaire* left standing as a predicate adjective. To volunteer, in those languages, is to carry or offer oneself *as* a willing person; the willingness stays an attribute and never becomes an action.

Portuguese alone has made the verb, and made it reflexively: *voluntariar-se*, "to present oneself as a volunteer," with a full conjugation. It is the closest analogue in the set to what English does, and it still keeps the reflexive that English drops.

The English conversion is not isolated. The *-eer* class is one of the few English noun suffixes that verbs freely — *engineer*, *pioneer*, *profiteer*, *auctioneer*, and *volunteer* all convert without a derivational marker. Whatever else *-eer* is doing, it produces nouns that English is willing to treat as verbs.

---

## 5. The Inglisce forms

`Volontire` follows French rather than Latin in the stem, writing `o` where English writes `u`. The choice is consistent with the word's actual transmission: English received it from French *volontaire*, and the `u` of the received spelling is a later correction toward a Latin the word did not come through. Writing `o` records the route rather than the destination, and aligns the form with *volontaire* and *volontario*.

The ending is the system's regular treatment of /ɪr/ — `i` for the vowel, with the infinitive `volontier` against the finite `volontire` and the progressive `volontiering`. The noun takes the finite form, `þa volontire`, plural `þa volontiers`. The article is feminine, `volontire` being feminine by virtue of its stress falling away from the first syllable; the rule that assigns gender is treated elsewhere.

`Volontairy` is /ˈvɑlənˌtɛri/, and the spelling says so where English does not. The English `-ary` suffix is regularly /ɛri/ — *necessary*, *military*, *secretary*, *ordinary* — but it is written with `ar`, a sequence that spells /ɑr/ almost everywhere else in the language: *car*, *far*, *star*, *hard*, *garden*. The reader is asked to give `ar` one value in *voluntary* and another in *voluntarily*'s near neighbours, with nothing on the page to tell them which.

English already has a spelling for /ɛri/ and uses it freely: *airy*, *dairy*, *fairy*, *hairy*. *Airy* is itself a word pronounced exactly as this suffix is. `Volontairy` does nothing more than write the suffix the way English writes the same sound when it is not embedded in a Latin ending.

The stem is untouched, so the adjective and the noun stay visibly one family, and the `air` holds the word against French *volontaire* and against the *-āri-* that Spanish *voluntario* and Italian *volontario* keep intact.

There is a coincidence here worth recording. The Inglisce plural `volontiers` is identical, letter for letter, to the French adverb *volontiers*, "willingly, gladly" — itself from Late Latin *voluntāriē* with an analogical adverbial *-s*, and so from the same adjective. Two languages have arrived at the same string from the same root by entirely unrelated operations: French by adverb formation on the ablative, Inglisce by pluralising a noun.

The verb is not reflexive, following English against Portuguese and against the periphrases of French, Spanish, and Italian.
