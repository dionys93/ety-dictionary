# Notes

## The principle

The vowel digraph records **whether two words belong together**. Words sharing a root are spelled alike; words that merely rhyme are kept apart.

| | | Related? | Spellings |
|---|---|---|---|
| break | breach | yes — OE *brecan* | bréic : breic̃e |
| bleak | bleach | yes — PG \**blaikaz* | bleic : bleic̃e |
| wake | watch | yes — OE *wacan*, *wæccan* | uâc : uac̃e |
| reek | reach | no — OE *rēocan* : *rǣcan* | riec : reic̃e |
| peach | impeach | no — OF *pesche* : *impedicāre* | poic̃e : impeic̃e |

*Impeach* is Late Latin *impedicāre*, "to catch by the foot," from *pēs*. Its relatives are *impede*, *pedal*, and *pedestrian*.

## Note A — `a` after `u`

Middle English *a* after /w/ rounded to /ɒ/ **except before a velar**, where it stayed /æ/.

| Environment | Outcome | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| /w/ + a + velar | /æ/ | *whack*, *quack*, *wax*, *wag* |
| /w/ + a + other | /ɑ/ | *watch*, *want*, *wash*, *swan* |
| /w/ + a + r | /ɔ/ | *war*, *warm*, *ward* |

So `uac̃e` is /wɑtʃ/ unmarked, `uac` would be /wæk/, and `uâc` takes its circumflex to separate /eɪ/ from /æ/.

## Note B — the /ɛtʃ/ vowel

`Ai` records a historical **a**, fronted or umlauted; plain `e` records a historical **e** or **i**. The set sorts cleanly:

| Inglisce | English | Source vowel |
|---|---|---|
| caic̃e | catch | Anglo-Norman *cachier*, VL \**captiāre* — **a** |
| aic̃e | etch | German *ätzen*, PG \**atjaną* — **ä** |
| faic̃e | fetch | OE *fetian*, \**fatjan* — **a** |
| caic̃e | ketch | ME *cacchen*, the same word as *catch* — **a** |
| raic̃e | retch | OE *hrǣcan* — **ǣ** |
| schec̃e | sketch | Italian *schizzo*, Dutch *schets* — **i**, **e** |
| strec̃e | stretch | OE *streccan* — **e** |
| rec̃e | wretch | OE *wrecca* — **e** |
| lec̃e | letch | OF *lechier* — **e** |
| vec̃e | vetch | Latin *vicia* — **i** |

**Etch is the key.** German *ätzen* is the causative of *essen* — Proto-Germanic \**atjaną* from \**etaną*, "to make eat," hence to bite into metal with acid. So *etch* and *eat* are the same root, and the `ai` records the *a* that the umlaut fronted.

**Ketch and catch are one word.** A ketch is a *catch-boat* — Middle English *cache*, from *cacchen* "to catch," used of a fishing vessel from the fifteenth century. The two are therefore spelled alike, and the homography is not a cost of the system but a requirement of it.

They are also easy to keep apart in use. One is among the commonest verbs in the language and the other a rare nautical noun; the reader who meets *a two-masted caic̃e* is in no doubt.

**Kvetch is the one outlier.** Yiddish *kvetshn* is German *quetschen*, from Middle High German *quetzen*, which has **e** rather than **a**. It may have been drawn into the `ai` group by shape rather than descent.

## Note C — `ai` as the lax partner

`Ai` also serves as the shortened form of two tense vowels, where English shortens the vowel in a derived word:

| Tense | Lax |
|---|---|
| `ie` = /i/ | `ai` = /ɛ/ |
| `éi` = /eɪ/ | `ai` = /ɛ/ |

So miete : mait, pliese : plaisant, hieve : haivy, and bréic : braicfast.

The alternation is applied where the etymology supports it, not mechanically. `Ie` is therefore reserved for words that have or might have an /ɛ/ partner — *keep*, *feel*, *mean*, *deep*, *breathe*, *serene* and their kind — and `ei` is free for the words that do not: *seek*, *speak*, *teach*, *beach*, *leak*, *bleak*, *eagle*.

`Ei` has English precedent for /i/ in *ceiling*, *receive*, *deceive*, *conceive*, *seize*, *either*, *weird*, *protein*, and *caffeine*.

## Note D — how far relatedness reaches

The principle marks relations that are still felt, not every relation that is historically true. Two decisions set the threshold.

**Black is blac.** Old English *blæc* "black" and *blāc* "pale, shining" are one root gone in opposite directions, so *black*, *bleak*, and *bleach* are technically a family. But the senses parted a thousand years ago and no speaker connects them, so blac stands apart from bleic and bleic̃e.

**Beseech is besoic̃e.** It is *be-* plus the southern form of *sēcan*, so it is *seek* with a prefix. But the two have diverged far enough that besoic̃e takes the ordinary treatment of an English `ee` rather than matching seic.

The test is not whether a dictionary can trace the link but whether the link does any work for a reader. Where it does — *break* and *breach*, *wake* and *watch*, *speak* and *speech* — the spelling shows it. Where it does not, the words go their own way.

## Note E — `c` and `ch` before an ending

The digraph appears exactly where a front vowel would otherwise soften the `c`:

| | Ending | Form |
|---|---|---|
| comparative | `-ar` | blacar — `a` is back, `c` is already /k/ |
| superlative | `-est` | blachest — `e` would soften, so `ch` |
| progressive | `-ing` | seiching, teic̃ing |
| present | `-s` | seics |

Seic and blac take the bare `c` because nothing follows, and `ch` appears only before `e` or `i`.

## Note F — the irregular pasts

The irregular pasts spell their vowels etymologically, so two verbs that rhyme in the past are written differently:

| | Past | Old English | Vowel |
|---|---|---|---|
| seek | soht | *sōhte* | **ō** |
| teach | tauht | *tāhte* | **ā** |
| wake | uóc | *wōc* | **ō** |
| take | tôc | *tōc* | **ō** |
| speak | spóc | *spræc*, *sprǣcon* | |
| break | bróc | *bræc*, *brǣcon* | |
| shake | seôc | *scēoc* | **ēo** |
| forsake | forsôc | *forsōc* | **ō** |

*Sought* and *taught* are /sɔt/ and /tɔt/ in speech; soht and tauht record the different vowels behind them.

## Note G — the `-eox` plural

The nouns in `ô` take `-eox` in the plural, so the singular and plural do not share a vowel spelling:

| Singular | Plural |
|---|---|
| bôc | beox |
| brôc | breox |
| côc | queox |
| crôc | creox |
| hôc | heox |
| lôc | leox |
| nôc | neox |
| rhoc | rheox |
| rôc | reox |
| snôc | sneox |

This restores an alternation English lost. Old English *bōc* had the plural *bēc*, the vowel fronted by i-mutation, and the same held across the class. Modern English levelled the paradigm to *book* : *books*; the `ô` : `eo` alternation writes the old distinction back while both remain /ʊ/ in speech.

**Queox shows the consonant rule.** A `c` before `e` would be /s/, so *cook* takes `qu` in the plural where the other nouns keep `c` before the back vowel of the singular.

## Note H — `ô` against `û`

Both are /ʊ/, and the difference is which Old English vowel is behind them.

| | Inglisce | Old English | Sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| brook (n.) | brôc | *brōc* | a stream |
| brook (v.) | brûc | *brūcan* | to tolerate |

The two are unrelated words that English spells alike. *Brūcan* meant "to use, to enjoy," and its Latin cognate is *fruī*, *frūctus* — so **the verb *brook* and the noun *fruit* are the same root**, and neither has anything to do with a stream.

## Note I — `rh` for a lost *h*

*Rook* the bird is Old English *hrōc*, with the *hr-* cluster that English lost in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries — the same loss that gave *ring* from *hring*, *raven* from *hræfn*, and *loaf* from *hlāf*.

Rhoc records the consonant that was there. *Rook* the chess piece is Persian *rukh* by way of Arabic, Medieval Latin *rocus*, and Old French *roc*, with no *h* at any stage, and takes rôc.

The verb *to rook* and *rookie* both descend from the bird — the bird's reputation gave the sense "swindler" — and so take rh- with it.

## Note K — the \*pikk- family

Two Proto-Germanic bases, and English merged them.

| | Base A — \**pikkōną* | Base B — \**pīk* |
|---|---|---|
| sense | to knock, peck, prick | a pointed object |
| Old English | \**pician*, \**piccean* | *pīc* |
| through Francia | Frankish \**pikkōn* → VL \**pīccare* → OF *pic*, *piquer* | — |
| English words | pick, peck, pitch, pique, pike (the weapon) | pike (spike, fish), peak |

Base B is the collateral long-vowel form of base A, so the two are ablaut variants of one root — related, but a step apart. *Pick*, *peck*, *pitch*, and *pique* are doublets in the strict sense: one Germanic verb, four English words, three inherited and one that went to Francia and came back through French.

**Pike is both.** The native *pīc* gave the spike and the fish; the infantry weapon of the 1510s is French *pique* borrowed outright. The two lines had already merged by then, both meaning a sharp point and both sounding alike.

| English | Inglisce | Why |
|---|---|---|
| pick | pic | base A, native |
| peck | pec | base A, native |
| pitch | pic̃e | base A, native |
| pique | pique | base A, through France |
| pike | pîque | base A weapon sense, merged with the native |
| peak | peic | base B, native only — no French route to record |
| prick | pric | WGmc \**prikojan* — the `r` was never in \**pikkōną* |
| peek | piec | ME *piken*, origin obscure; separated from peic |

`Qu` marks the words that went through France. `Peak` cannot take it, being a 1520s variant of the native *pike* that never left England, and so falls back on the default `ei`.

**Neither the vowel nor the consonant frame sorts this family.** Four base-A words carry four different vowels, and `p_c` covers *pick*, *peck*, and *peek* alike — one outsider inside the frame. The family is a root scattered across vowels, and no spelling can unify it without misreporting the sound. What the spelling can do is record the route, which is what `qu` does.

## Note J — the `-x` plural

Where the singular ends in a velar, the plural writes `-x` for /ks/ rather than `-cs`:

| Singular | Plural |
|---|---|
| râc | raix |
| câc | caix |
| bréic | bréix |
| șreic | șreix |
| foac | foax |
| cuac | cuax |

The `-eox` plurals of note G are the same device with the older vowel.

The conditioning appears to be the **plural's own vowel**, not the singular's:

| Plural vowel | Ending | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| digraph | `-x` | raix, caix, bréix, șreix, foax, cuax, beox |
| single letter | `-cs` | sacs, lâcs, bîcs, pícs |

`Câc` : `caix` fits — single `â` in the singular, digraph `ai` in the plural, and the plural governs. `Bîc` : `bîcs` fits, single `î` throughout.

**`Pîx` is the exception.** By the rule it should be `pîcs`. The likely reason is that `pícs` and `pîcs` would then differ by one accent on a pair the system is deliberately keeping apart, and `pícs` : `pîx` separates them properly — but it means `-x` is doing a second job.

## Note L — two stress tests

**Bike is `bîc`.** The word is a clipping of *bicycle*, so its vowel is Greek *bi-* through Latin rather than a native diphthong, and `bîc` : `bîcycle` keeps the relation. The `c` softens before `y` in the full form, which is wanted there — unlike `blachest`, where the softening had to be blocked.

`Bîc` and `pîque` are now separated by the consonant alone, both being /aɪk/ with a circumflex. That is correct, the words being unrelated, and it puts weight on the `c` : `qu` distinction. Most of the /aɪk/ slot will take `c` — *dike* is OE *dīc*, *strike* is OE *strīcan* — so `qu` stays rare and meaningful.

**Peace is `peice` and piece is unchanged.** The two are unrelated: *peace* is Latin *pācem*, from \**peh₂ǵ-* "to fasten," the family of *pact*, *pagan*, and *propagate*; *piece* is Gaulish \**pettia* "a bit," through Medieval Latin *pecia*.

`Peice` takes the default and `piece` keeps its English spelling as the separator. `Ie` is not reserved for alternating pairs — note C applies it where the etymology supports it, not mechanically — so a word using `ie` without an /ɛ/ partner is within the rule.

## Still open

**The `-que` forms.** Lique, mique, pique are the three /ik/ words with no relative in the set — nothing to match and nothing to avoid. If that is the logic, `-que` marks the words the network does not reach. *Lâque* and *puque* now show the same ending in other slots.

**Pairs still to decide.** *week* : *weak*, *creek* : *creak*, *meet* : *meat*, *see* : *sea*, *heel* : *heal*, *steel* : *steal*, *beat* : *beet*, *peel* : *peal* — all unrelated, all needing separation.

**Shake and shook.** The forms are seac and seôc, where the /ʃ/ is written `se` rather than the `sc` or `ș` used elsewhere. Old English *sceacan* and *scēoc* have the `sc`.

**The `-c` / `-ch` proposal.** With `-c` as the infinitive, a noun-verb pair loses the silent `-e` that distinguishes them elsewhere. *Cook*, *hook*, *look*, *check*, *track*, *back*, *pack*, *crack*, and *smoke* are all both.
