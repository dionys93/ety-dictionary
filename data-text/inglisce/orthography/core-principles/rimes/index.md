## Needs establishing

### 1. How /ʃ/ is written before a vowel

Four spellings appear for one sound, and the conditioning is not stated:

| Form | English | Spelling of /ʃ/ |
|---|---|---|
| siac | shack | `si` |
| cioc | shock | `ci` |
| seac | shake | `se` |
| seuc | shuck | `se` |
| seôc | shook | `se` |
| șreic | shriek | `ș` |
| șéic | sheik | `ș` |

`Ș` appears before a consonant and the others before a vowel, which may be the rule — but `si`, `se`, and `ci` are then three spellings for the same position. Old English *sceacan*, *sceoc*, and *sceacca* all have `sc`.

### 2. `eu` against `u` in /ʌk/

| `eu` | plain `u` |
|---|---|
| beuc, meuc, seuc, treuc | pluc, suc, tuc |
| steuc, streuc, sneuc | |

The last three are ablaut pasts, where `eu` is clearly the past-tense vowel. The first four are not pasts, and what puts *buck*, *muck*, *shuck*, and *truck* in that group rather than with *pluck*, *suck*, and *tuck* is not yet said.

### 3. `ec` or `eque` in /ɛk/

The section header asks the question. `Dec`, `nec`, `pec`, `hec`, and `r̃ec` take `c`; *check* is given both ways.

### 4. Three graphemes appearing once each

| | Form | English | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| x̃ | uix̃e | witch | separator only, or does it record OE *wicce*'s geminate? |
| gn | gnoc | knock | OE *cnucian* has `cn`, not `gn` |
| hl | hluc | luck | Middle Dutch *luc* has no `hl` |

### 5. The accent inventory

`Â î ô û ó í é ò è` are all in use. What each marks is clear word by word but has not been set out as a table, and `ô` currently covers both /ʊ/ and, in `hòc`, something else.

### 6. Whether `-oque` and `-oac` are one paradigm or two

The /oʊk/ slot now shows `oqu` before a vowel letter and `oac` where none follows — `joque` : `joac` : `joqued` : `joquing`, the same constraint that gives `convoque` : `convoac` and `uirche` : `uirc`.

But three nouns take `oac` throughout and have no verb: `bloac`, `foac`, `spoac`, `moac`. If those ever become verbs they would need `bloque`, `foque`, `spoque`. Whether `oac` is the noun form or merely the bare form is the open question.

`Creoc` is the outlier: *croak* is a verb and takes `creoc` rather than `croque`.

# Etymological clusters

## What the /k/ ~ /tʃ/ divide actually is

The two slots are not two sets of words. For the native stratum they are **one consonant in two environments**.

Old English *c* was /k/ before a back vowel and /tʃ/ before a front vowel, and the palatalisation was automatic. Every word that had both environments in its paradigm ended up with both consonants, and English later split them into separate words:

| /k/ | /tʃ/ | One Old English word |
|---|---|---|
| speak — speic | speech — speic̃e | *specan* : *sprǣc* |
| break — bréic | breach — breic̃e | *brecan* : *bryce* |
| bake — bâc | batch — bac̃e | *bacan* : \**bæcce* |
| wake — uâc | watch — uac̃e | *wacan* : *wæccan* |
| dike — dîc | ditch — dic̃e | *dīc* : *dīc* |
| stick — stic | stitch — stic̃e | *stician* : *stice* |
| book — bôc | beech — boic̃e | *bōc* : *bēce* |
| make — mâc | match — mac̃e | *macian* : *gemæcca* |
| seek — seic | beseech — besoic̃e | *sēcan* : *besēcan* |
| cheek — c̃eic | choke — c̃eoc | *cēace* : *ācēocian* |
| thatch — þac̃e | deck — dec | \**þakją*, one through Dutch |

**This is the assumption the whole divide rests on.** `C` and `c̃` are one letter with a mark because /k/ and /tʃ/ are one consonant with a history. The system is not pairing two arbitrary sounds; it is writing an alternation Old English had and English lost.

**And the assumption fails outside the native stratum.** Three other things also produce /tʃ/ in English:

| Source | Words | Marked how |
|---|---|---|
| Latin *c* through Norman French | catch, ketch | c̃ |
| Latin or Germanic *t* + yod | Dutch, nature, furniture | ț |
| Germanic *sk* | — none in these slots | sc elsewhere |

So `c̃` is doing two jobs — the native palatalisation and the French one — and `ț` was invented to keep the third out of it. That division is stated in note M and holds, but it means `c̃` alone does not tell you which stratum a word belongs to.

## What the divide assumes about spelling

### 1. The affricate slot has no `qu`

`Qu` is /k/ by definition, so it cannot appear before a mark that makes the consonant an affricate. Every device the velar slot uses to record a French route — `pique`, `crique`, `attaque`, `joque` — is **unavailable in /tʃ/**.

The consequence: *catch* went through France and *chase* is its Central French twin, but nothing in `caic̃e` says so. In the velar slot that fact would be spelled.

### 2. The affricate slot has no `x`

`X` writes /ks/, which the affricate cannot produce. So `-x` plurals are a velar-only device, and every /tʃ/ noun pluralises the long way.

That also means note J's rule — digraph takes `-x`, single letter takes `-cs` — has no application in half the system.

### 3. The silent `-e` is near-obligatory after `c̃` and near-absent after `c`

| | With `-e` | Without |
|---|---|---|
| /tʃ/ | bic̃e, dic̃e, coc̃e, teic̃e, buc̃e | uic̃, ric̃, eic̃, moc̃, soc̃, duț |
| /k/ | — | bic, dic, coc, teic, buc |

The exceptions are all function words and adjectives, which is the noun-adjective rule stated elsewhere. But the asymmetry itself is an assumption: the affricate is treated as needing closure and the velar is not.

### 4. A doubled consonant marks a short vowel — but only in /ɑtʃ/

`Bocc̃e`, `crocc̃e`, `nocc̃e` double the `c` where `broc̃e`, `coc̃e`, `roc̃e` do not. The doubling separates /ɑtʃ/ from /oʊtʃ/, which the vowel letter alone cannot do.

This is English's own convention — *hopping* against *hoping* — applied inside the affricate slot only. The velar slot never doubles; it uses accents instead.

### 5. `Oa` changes value across the divide

| Before | Value |
|---|---|
| `c` | /oʊ/ — croac, foac, oac |
| `c̃` | /aʊ/ — croac̃e, poac̃e, oac̃e |

This is the only grapheme in the system whose value depends on the following consonant, and it exists because both slots needed a two-vowel spelling and only one could have the obvious one.

### 6. The /ʌ/ and /u/ spellings do carry across

`Eu` is /ʌ/ in both — `beuc`, `c̃euc` beside `cleuc̃e`, `heuc̃e`. `Ou` is /u/ in both — `flouc`, `snouc` beside `houc̃e`, `pouc̃e`. Those two vowels are the only ones that behave identically on both sides.

**Chalk is calcium.** Latin *calx*, *calcis* "limestone" was borrowed into Old English as *cealc*, where the *c* palatalised before the front vowel — so `c̃auc` and *calcium*, *calculate*, *calcify* are one word, the first through speech and the rest from the page. A *calculus* was a pebble used for counting.

**Cook is kitchen.** Latin *coquere* gave both *cocus* "a cook" and *coquīna* "a kitchen," and Old English took both — *cōc* with the velar and *cycene* with the palatal. So `côc` and *kitchen* are the same divide as *bake* and *batch*, and *cuisine*, *biscuit*, *precocious*, and *apricot* are the same verb again.

**Catch and chase are a doublet the system cannot show.** Vulgar Latin \**captiāre* gave Norman *cachier* and Central French *chacier*; English took both. `Caic̃e` and *chase* have the same relation as *ward* and *guard* — and where the velar slot could mark it with `qu`, the affricate slot has no device.

**Rack and rake are the *rectus* root.** PIE \**h₃reǵ-* "to move in a straight line" gives *rack* through Middle Dutch *rec* "something stretched out," and *rake* through OE *raca*, the tool that draws a straight line through soil. The same root gives *right*, *rule*, *regal*, *reign*, *direct*, and — through Celtic \**rīx* — **rich**. So `rac`, `râc`, and `ric` belong with `roiht` and `reule`.

**Stack, stake, attack, and attach are one stick.** PG \**stakon-* went to Romance as Spanish *estaca* and Old French *estache*; English took it back as *attach*, and Italian *attaccare* gave *attack*. Four words, and the system marks the returned pair with `qu` and `c̃` while the native pair keeps plain `c`.

**Which, such, and each all end in OE *-līc*.** *Hwilc* is *hwā* + *līc*, *swylc* is *swā* + *līc*, *ǣlc* is *ā* + *ge-līc* — the second element being the word that became *like*. `Uic̃`, `soc̃`, `eic̃`, and `lîc` share a morpheme no speaker can see, and the forms do not show it. The compounds were opaque by 1200.

# Notes

## Note O — when `ei` is /i/ and when it is /eɪ/

`Ei` is /i/ in three environments and /eɪ/ everywhere else.

| Environment | Value | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| word-initial | /i/ | eigle, eisy, eite, eic̃ |
| after `s`, `c`, `c̃` | /i/ | seise, seie, seide, ceiling, conceile, c̃eise |
| before `c`, `c̃` | /i/ | beic, seic, teic, ueic, reic̃e, speic̃e |
| **elsewhere** | **/eɪ/** | **veine, obteine, conveie** |

The first two environments are the schoolroom rule about *i* before *e* stated as a condition rather than a superstition, and the third extends it to the velar and the affricate.

**The acute is an override, not a decoration.** `Bréic` and `stéic` are /eɪ/ words sitting in a /i/ environment — `ei` before `c` — so they need the mark. `Éime` is /eɪ/ word-initially and needs it for the same reason. Everywhere else the environment does the work and no accent appears, so `veine` and `obteine` are unmarked.

That keeps `bréic` : `breic̃e` intact, which is the pair the whole *break* family hangs on.

### The four spellings of /i/

| Grapheme | When | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| `ei` | in the three environments above | beic, seise, eigle |
| `i…e` | where `ei` is not licensed | uide, dide, recive |
| `ie` | with a lax partner, or as a separator | diep : daipþ; piec, uiec |
| `í` | where no silent `-e` is available | díds, cantíns, pícs |

Each has a stated trigger. Only `ie` requires a judgement.

**One asymmetry to watch.** `Éime` takes the acute word-initially and `eiht` does not, though both are /eɪ/ and both begin the word. Either `ht` licenses /eɪ/ on its own or *eight* is an exception.

## Note A — `a` after `u`

Middle English *a* after /w/ rounded to /ɒ/ **except before a velar**, where it stayed /æ/.

| Environment | Outcome | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| /w/ + a + velar | /æ/ | *whack*, *quack*, *wax*, *wag* |
| /w/ + a + other | /ɑ/ | *watch*, *want*, *wash*, *swan* |
| /w/ + a + r | /ɔ/ | *war*, *warm*, *ward* |

So `uac̃e` is /wɑtʃ/ unmarked, `uac` would be /wæk/, and `uâc` takes its circumflex to separate /eɪ/ from /æ/.

## Note B — the /ɛtʃ/ vowel

`Ai` records a historical **a**, fronted or umlauted; plain `e` records a historical **e** or **i**. The set sorts cleanly:

| Inglisce | English | Source vowel |
|---|---|---|
| caic̃e | catch | Anglo-Norman *cachier*, VL \**captiāre* — **a** |
| aic̃e | etch | German *ätzen*, PG \**atjaną* — **ä** |
| faic̃e | fetch | OE *fetian*, \**fatjan* — **a** |
| caic̃e | ketch | ME *cacchen*, the same word as *catch* — **a** |
| raic̃e | retch | OE *hrǣcan* — **ǣ** |
| schec̃e | sketch | Italian *schizzo*, Dutch *schets* — **i**, **e** |
| strec̃e | stretch | OE *streccan* — **e** |
| rec̃e | wretch | OE *wrecca* — **e** |
| lec̃e | letch | OF *lechier* — **e** |
| vec̃e | vetch | Latin *vicia* — **i** |

**Etch is the key.** German *ätzen* is the causative of *essen* — Proto-Germanic \**atjaną* from \**etaną*, "to make eat," hence to bite into metal with acid. So *etch* and *eat* are the same root, and the `ai` records the *a* that the umlaut fronted.

**Ketch and catch are one word.** A ketch is a *catch-boat* — Middle English *cache*, from *cacchen* "to catch," used of a fishing vessel from the fifteenth century. The two are therefore spelled alike, and the homography is not a cost of the system but a requirement of it.

They are also easy to keep apart in use. One is among the commonest verbs in the language and the other a rare nautical noun; the reader who meets *a two-masted caic̃e* is in no doubt.

**Kvetch is the one outlier.** Yiddish *kvetshn* is German *quetschen*, from Middle High German *quetzen*, which has **e** rather than **a**. It may have been drawn into the `ai` group by shape rather than descent.

## Note C — `ai` as the lax partner

`Ai` also serves as the shortened form of two tense vowels, where English shortens the vowel in a derived word:

| Tense | Lax |
|---|---|
| `ie` = /i/ | `ai` = /ɛ/ |
| `éi` = /eɪ/ | `ai` = /ɛ/ |

So miete : mait, pliese : plaisant, hieve : haivy, and bréic : braicfast.

The alternation is applied where the etymology supports it, not mechanically. `Ie` is therefore reserved for words that have or might have an /ɛ/ partner — *keep*, *feel*, *mean*, *deep*, *breathe*, *serene* and their kind — and `ei` is free for the words that do not: *seek*, *speak*, *teach*, *beach*, *leak*, *bleak*, *eagle*.

`Ei` has English precedent for /i/ in *ceiling*, *receive*, *deceive*, *conceive*, *seize*, *either*, *weird*, *protein*, and *caffeine*.

## Note D — how far relatedness reaches

The principle marks relations that are still felt, not every relation that is historically true. Two decisions set the threshold.

**Black is blac.** Old English *blæc* "black" and *blāc* "pale, shining" are one root gone in opposite directions, so *black*, *bleak*, and *bleach* are technically a family. But the senses parted a thousand years ago and no speaker connects them, so blac stands apart from bleic and bleic̃e.

**Beseech is besoic̃e.** It is *be-* plus the southern form of *sēcan*, so it is *seek* with a prefix. But the two have diverged far enough that besoic̃e takes the ordinary treatment of an English `ee` rather than matching seic.

The test is not whether a dictionary can trace the link but whether the link does any work for a reader. Where it does — *break* and *breach*, *wake* and *watch*, *speak* and *speech* — the spelling shows it. Where it does not, the words go their own way.

## Note E — `c` and `ch` before an ending

The digraph appears exactly where a front vowel would otherwise soften the `c`:

| | Ending | Form |
|---|---|---|
| comparative | `-ar` | blacar — `a` is back, `c` is already /k/ |
| superlative | `-est` | blachest — `e` would soften, so `ch` |
| progressive | `-ing` | seiching, teic̃ing |
| present | `-s` | seics |

Seic and blac take the bare `c` because nothing follows, and `ch` appears only before `e` or `i`.

## Note F — the irregular pasts

The irregular pasts spell their vowels etymologically, so two verbs that rhyme in the past are written differently:

| | Past | Old English | Vowel |
|---|---|---|---|
| seek | soht | *sōhte* | **ō** |
| teach | tauht | *tāhte* | **ā** |
| wake | uóc | *wōc* | **ō** |
| take | tôc | *tōc* | **ō** |
| speak | spóc | *spræc*, *sprǣcon* | |
| break | bróc | *bræc*, *brǣcon* | |
| shake | seôc | *scēoc* | **ēo** |
| forsake | forsôc | *forsōc* | **ō** |

*Sought* and *taught* are /sɔt/ and /tɔt/ in speech; soht and tauht record the different vowels behind them.

## Note G — the `-eox` plural

The nouns in `ô` take `-eox` in the plural, so the singular and plural do not share a vowel spelling:

| Singular | Plural |
|---|---|
| bôc | beox |
| brôc | breox |
| côc | queox |
| crôc | creox |
| hôc | heox |
| lôc | leox |
| nôc | neox |
| rhoc | rheox |
| rôc | reox |
| snôc | sneox |

This restores an alternation English lost. Old English *bōc* had the plural *bēc*, the vowel fronted by i-mutation, and the same held across the class. Modern English levelled the paradigm to *book* : *books*; the `ô` : `eo` alternation writes the old distinction back while both remain /ʊ/ in speech.

**Queox shows the consonant rule.** A `c` before `e` would be /s/, so *cook* takes `qu` in the plural where the other nouns keep `c` before the back vowel of the singular.

## Note H — `ô` against `û`

Both are /ʊ/, and the difference is which Old English vowel is behind them.

| | Inglisce | Old English | Sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| brook (n.) | brôc | *brōc* | a stream |
| brook (v.) | brûc | *brūcan* | to tolerate |

The two are unrelated words that English spells alike. *Brūcan* meant "to use, to enjoy," and its Latin cognate is *fruī*, *frūctus* — so **the verb *brook* and the noun *fruit* are the same root**, and neither has anything to do with a stream.

## Note I — `rh` for a lost *h*

*Rook* the bird is Old English *hrōc*, with the *hr-* cluster that English lost in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries — the same loss that gave *ring* from *hring*, *raven* from *hræfn*, and *loaf* from *hlāf*.

Rhoc records the consonant that was there. *Rook* the chess piece is Persian *rukh* by way of Arabic, Medieval Latin *rocus*, and Old French *roc*, with no *h* at any stage, and takes rôc.

The verb *to rook* and *rookie* both descend from the bird — the bird's reputation gave the sense "swindler" — and so take rh- with it.

## Note M — which letter carries /tʃ/

Three graphemes give the affricate, and each records the consonant it descends from — the same principle that governs `ș` from *s* and `d̦` from *d*.

| Grapheme | From | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| c̃ | a historical *c* or *k* | caic̃e, ric̃e, coc̃e, uic̃ |
| ț | a historical *t* | duț, fournițure |
| ch | a Germanic dorsal, read /k/ | chic̃e |

**Duț is *Deutsch*.** The word is Middle Dutch *duutsch*, German *Deutsch*, and the affricate there descends from a *t*, not a *c*. Writing `ț` records that, and puts *Dutch* with *nature* and *furniture* rather than with *catch* and *rich*.

**This is why ketch is `caic̃e` and not `caiț`.** Middle English *cacchen* has a geminate *c*; no *t* was ever in it.

**Kitsch is `chic̃e`.** German *Kitsch* begins with a dorsal /k/, so the initial takes `ch`; the affricate at the end takes `c̃`.

## Note N — `oa` before the affricate

The digraph `oa` is /oʊ/ generally, but **`oac̃` is always /aʊ/**. That resolves what would otherwise be six collisions:

| /oʊtʃ/ | /aʊtʃ/ |
|---|---|
| coc̃e — coach | coac̃e — couch |
| poc̃e — poach | poac̃e — pouch |
| broc̃e — broach | croac̃e — crouch |
| roc̃e — roach | groac̃e — grouch |

The `a` is the only difference, and it is enough because the environment is fixed.

## Note K — the \*pikk- family

Two Proto-Germanic bases, and English merged them.

| | Base A — \**pikkōną* | Base B — \**pīk* |
|---|---|---|
| sense | to knock, peck, prick | a pointed object |
| Old English | \**pician*, \**piccean* | *pīc* |
| through Francia | Frankish \**pikkōn* → VL \**pīccare* → OF *pic*, *piquer* | — |
| English words | pick, peck, pitch, pique, pike (the weapon) | pike (spike, fish), peak |

Base B is the collateral long-vowel form of base A, so the two are ablaut variants of one root — related, but a step apart. *Pick*, *peck*, *pitch*, and *pique* are doublets in the strict sense: one Germanic verb, four English words, three inherited and one that went to Francia and came back through French.

**Pike is both.** The native *pīc* gave the spike and the fish; the infantry weapon of the 1510s is French *pique* borrowed outright. The two lines had already merged by then, both meaning a sharp point and both sounding alike.

| English | Inglisce | Why |
|---|---|---|
| pick | pic | base A, native |
| peck | pec | base A, native |
| pitch | pic̃e | base A, native |
| pique | pique | base A, through France |
| pike | pîque | base A weapon sense, merged with the native |
| peak | peic | base B, native only — no French route to record |
| prick | pric | WGmc \**prikojan* — the `r` was never in \**pikkōną* |
| peek | piec | ME *piken*, origin obscure; separated from peic |

`Qu` marks the words that went through France. `Peak` cannot take it, being a 1520s variant of the native *pike* that never left England, and so falls back on the default `ei`.

**Neither the vowel nor the consonant frame sorts this family.** Four base-A words carry four different vowels, and `p_c` covers *pick*, *peck*, and *peek* alike — one outsider inside the frame. The family is a root scattered across vowels, and no spelling can unify it without misreporting the sound. What the spelling can do is record the route, which is what `qu` does.

## Note J — the `-x` plural

Where the singular ends in a velar, the plural writes `-x` for /ks/ rather than `-cs`:

| Singular | Plural |
|---|---|
| râc | raix |
| câc | caix |
| bréic | bréix |
| șreic | șreix |
| foac | foax |
| cuac | cuax |

The `-eox` plurals of note G are the same device with the older vowel.

The conditioning appears to be the **plural's own vowel**, not the singular's:

| Plural vowel | Ending | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| digraph | `-x` | raix, caix, bréix, șreix, foax, cuax, beox |
| single letter | `-cs` | sacs, lâcs, bîcs, pícs |

`Câc` : `caix` fits — single `â` in the singular, digraph `ai` in the plural, and the plural governs. `Bîc` : `bîcs` fits, single `î` throughout.

**`Pîx` is the exception.** By the rule it should be `pîcs`. The likely reason is that `pícs` and `pîcs` would then differ by one accent on a pair the system is deliberately keeping apart, and `pícs` : `pîx` separates them properly — but it means `-x` is doing a second job.

## Note L — two stress tests

**Bike is `bîc`.** The word is a clipping of *bicycle*, so its vowel is Greek *bi-* through Latin rather than a native diphthong, and `bîc` : `bîcycle` keeps the relation. The `c` softens before `y` in the full form, which is wanted there — unlike `blachest`, where the softening had to be blocked.

`Bîc` and `pîque` are now separated by the consonant alone, both being /aɪk/ with a circumflex. That is correct, the words being unrelated, and it puts weight on the `c` : `qu` distinction. Most of the /aɪk/ slot will take `c` — *dike* is OE *dīc*, *strike* is OE *strīcan* — so `qu` stays rare and meaningful.

**Peace is `peice` and piece is unchanged.** The two are unrelated: *peace* is Latin *pācem*, from \**peh₂ǵ-* "to fasten," the family of *pact*, *pagan*, and *propagate*; *piece* is Gaulish \**pettia* "a bit," through Medieval Latin *pecia*.

`Peice` takes the default and `piece` keeps its English spelling as the separator. `Ie` is not reserved for alternating pairs — note C applies it where the etymology supports it, not mechanically — so a word using `ie` without an /ɛ/ partner is within the rule.

## Note P — the three tiers of /ik/

| Tier | Form | When |
|---|---|---|
| default | `-eic` | nothing forces otherwise |
| separator | `-iec` | a homophone in the same slot |
| class marker | `-ique` | a Romance-shaped adjective, or a noun with a French route |

**The separator tier is exact.** Every `-iec` word has an `-eic` twin:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| uiec | ueic | week : weak |
| liec | leic | leek : leak |
| riec | reic̃e | reek : reach |
| piec | peic | peek : peak |

**And so is `-oic̃e` in the affricate slot**, which does the same job:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| boic̃e | beic̃e | beech : beach |
| broic̃e | breic̃e | breech : breach |
| loic̃e | leic̃e | leech : leach |
| poic̃e | empeic̃e | peach : impeach |
| besoic̃e | seic | beseech : seek |

**`-ique` is a word class, not a third separator.** *Antique*, *unique*, *boutique*, *oblique*, *pique* are English's Romance adjective ending, and `mique` and `slique` join it by shape. A noun takes it only with a French route to show — `crique` from Old French *crique*, `clique` and `pique` from French outright.

That is why *creek* is `crique` and not `criec` even though `creic` exists to be separated from: the French route outranks the separator.

**Sleek and slick are one word.** Middle English *slike*, treated by the OED as variants of a single adjective. `Slique` takes the Romance shape and `slic` the plain one; no third form is needed.

## Note Q — the /oʊk/ paradigm

One grapheme, two shapes, conditioned by what follows:

| Position | Spelling | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| before a vowel letter | `oqu` | joque, joqued, joquing, smoque |
| bare, or before `-s`, `-x` | `oac` | joac, joacs, cloax, yoax |

So `cloque` and `cloac` are not alternatives but one paradigm — the citation form and the bare stem.

**Three entries are doing relatedness work.**

`C̃eoc` for *choke*. Old English *ācēocian* is built on *cēoce* "jaw," which is the *cheek* word — to choke is to seize by the jaw. `C̃eic` : `c̃eoc` shows a relation English hides entirely.

`Cloque` beside `cloc`. Medieval Latin *clocca* "bell" gave both *clock* and *cloak*, the garment named for the shape it makes from the shoulders. Old French used *cloche* for both.

`Eoc` against `yoque`. *Yolk* is OE *ġeolca* from *ġeolu* "yellow"; *yoke* is OE *ġeoc* from PIE \**yugóm*, the *join* word. Both begin *ġeo-* in Old English and are unrelated, so the separation is required.

**The silent `l` is gone throughout.** `Foac`, `eoc`, `tauc`, `uauc`, `c̃auc`, `stauc`, `bauc`, `cauc` — eight words, no `l` in any, and no exception in the data.

**Coke is `coac` because the alternatives are taken.** `Coc` is /kɑk/, and `cóc` would be an accent-only pair with `côc`. The default was the only form available, which is the tidiest kind of decision.

## Still open

**Pairs still to decide.** *meet* : *meat*, *see* : *sea*, *heel* : *heal*, *steel* : *steal*, *beat* : *beet*, *peel* : *peal* — all unrelated, all needing separation.

**Shake and shook.** The forms are seac and seôc, where the /ʃ/ is written `se` rather than the `sc` or `ș` used elsewhere. Old English *sceacan* and *scēoc* have the `sc`.

**Witch is `uix̃e`.** The `x̃` appears nowhere else and separates *witch* from *which*, `uic̃`. Whether it is a dedicated separator or records something about OE *wicce*'s geminate is not yet stated.

**The `-c` / `-ch` proposal.** With `-c` as the infinitive, a noun-verb pair loses the silent `-e` that distinguishes them elsewhere. *Cook*, *hook*, *look*, *check*, *track*, *back*, *pack*, *crack*, and *smoke* are all both.

# Velars: state of the system

## /kw/

Conditioned by what follows. No etymology needed.

| Environment | Spelling | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| before *a*, *o* | `qu` | quac, quaic, quote, quarie, equâte, equâtre |
| before a marked *i* (î, í) | `qu` | quît, quîet, requîer, acquîer, bequíþe |
| before plain *e* | `cu` | frícuent, consecuent, sícuence |
| before plain *i*, initial | `cu` | cuic, cuine |
| before plain *i*, medial | `qü` | liqüide, sqüide |

`Qu` before an *i*-letter holds where a diacritic breaks the minim run.
Initial `cu` + *i* has room in the hand; medial does not.

## /kj/

Never marked. `Qu` holds /kw/ before back vowels, so `cu` there can only be /kj/.

| Case | Spelling | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| before a consonant, final, or silent `-e` | `cu` | cúe, cús |
| before a vowel, root-internal | `cu` | evacuait, evacuâcion |
| at a suffix boundary | `cu`, read from the base | rescuer, evacuee |
| stressed, where `u` could read /ʌ/ | `cú` | cús |

Root-internal `cu` + *e* is /kw/ without exception. Every /kj/ case in that
slot is a suffixed form of a base already marked by `ú` or by `-ait`.

## /gj/

`Gu` has no spare letter to take /gw/ off its hands the way `qu` does for `cu`,
so both readings are already occupied and the mark is needed.
Grounding: Spanish `ñ`, historically *n* + palatal.

| Case | Spelling | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| before a vowel | `g̃u` | arg̃ue, ambig̃ueus |
| before a consonant | `gu` | regular, figure |
| stressed | `gú` | the *ambiguity* type |

The two velars are not symmetric here, for a structural reason.

| Mark | Job |
|---|---|
| `ú`, `í`, `ó` | stressed, tense |
| `â` | /eɪ/; typed on the w key |

## Pronoun clitics

| Stem ends in | Form | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| a silent `-e` | apostrophe, elided | sihe'm |
| a consonant | hyphen, *h*- or *þ*- dropped | baic-im, tâc-im, taics-im, sihes-im |

`'M` covers both *him* and *them*; both are *h*-pronouns historically, since
`'em` is Middle English *hem*, not a clipping of *them*. The hyphenated form
names one.

## Open

- Stem vowel in the bare form: `baic` or `bâc`.
- `Oi` after `c` now carries three values: /ɪ/ (coic, coite), the /i/

- Verb *frequent*: adjective stress (frí-) or second-syllable stress.
- Bare `g` before *e*, *i*, *y* — presumably /dʒ/, not yet stated.
