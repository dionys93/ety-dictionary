**Monosyllables against polysyllables.** A monosyllable is stressed by definition — there is
no other syllable to take the stress — so a stress mark on one is vacuous.
§1 holds every monosyllable in the survey regardless of how it is spelled or
what vowel it has, because the syllable count is what the rule turns on.

**Within polysyllables, stressed against unstressed.** Here the final either carries the
stress or doesn't, so the mark has something to say. The acute is contrastive only in this
position: `beló`, `agló`, `alþó`, `agó`, `gonghó` against `bángalo`, `forbalo`, `mòro`,
`tomòro`. Every polysyllable is in §2 or §3, whatever its vowel.

Within those cuts, words are grouped by the pattern their Inglisce forms follow.

### 3.3 · Loan -o and the English -o suffix

Open and productive. The `-o` arrived already unstressed, or was added in English. Nouns take `-o`; verbs mostly take `-oe`, with the stress held on the root. A vowel before the final `-o` (`audio`, `ródio`) changes nothing. The verbs of *buffalo*, *stucco* and *torpedo* are the exceptions.
